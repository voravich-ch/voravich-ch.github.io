My AI research assistant

June 2026 · AI in research

People assume that when I say "AI-assisted trading system," the AI is the one placing trades. It isn't. In my setup, a language model never sizes a position, never sends an order, and never picks a strategy. I do that.

What I actually use it for is the grunt work I'd otherwise do myself every morning: reading through central-bank statements and data releases, summarizing what changed versus expectations, pulling up historical analogues, and pushing back when my own reasoning looks thin. Some mornings it disagrees with me, which is usually more useful than if it just agreed.

I keep it there on purpose. Nothing trades that I haven't pre-registered and signed off as a strategy, every order passes hard risk caps I set in advance, and the model has no path around either. It saves me hours of reading dense macro text every day. It doesn't get a vote on risk.

That's the whole design: I get through far more research than I could alone, and I still make every call that matters.

Fail-closed design in financial systems

June 2026 · systems design

Most software, when something breaks, just logs an error and keeps going. I don't build my trading tools that way, because the thing they touch is money.

If market data looks stale, an account identity doesn't match, or a reconciliation check doesn't tie out, I want the system to stop and block new risk rather than guess and hope I catch it later. Most days that means nothing happens, which is fine. Doing nothing is usually the right call when something looks off.

Building it this way took more effort than I expected. I had to sit down and actually list out the ways each piece could fail, then decide in advance what "safe" looks like for each one, instead of just handling the cases that came up naturally while coding.

The result is a bounded worst case. I can miss a trade because the system was overly cautious. What it can't easily do is keep running through a failure and quietly pile on risk.

A research result is only as good as its negative controls

June 2026 · methodology

A backtest that looks good on the first try doesn't tell me much. What I actually want to know is how hard I tried to break it before I believed it.

Most of my research hours go into trying to kill my own ideas, not confirming them. Before I trust a pattern, it has to survive walk-forward testing (pick parameters on old data, test on a slice it never saw) plus realistic transaction costs and a check that every decision only used information that actually existed on that date. I also run versions designed to fail: flip the direction, randomize the signal. If those somehow look profitable too, my test is broken, not my strategy good.

Since May I've logged more than 20 of these pre-registered tests: sizing rules, execution-cost models, macro overlays, a broad crypto benchmark. Most of them didn't survive, including that crypto benchmark, which found no validated edge — the rest of the crypto lane sits parked until fresh out-of-sample data says otherwise.

I used to think that was a bad month of research. Now I think it's the only kind of result worth trusting.

Why point-in-time macro data matters

June 2026 · methodology

Most macro data gets revised after it's first released (GDP, inflation, the numbers everyone quotes). If you backtest against today's final, revised figures, your model is quietly using information that didn't exist yet on the day it was supposedly deciding. That's look-ahead bias, and it makes results look better than they'd have actually been.

So I store point-in-time data instead: what was actually known on each date, not what we know now with hindsight. I keep FRED's vintage releases and rebuild the information set as of each decision date. It's more work, and honestly my backtests got worse the day I switched to it. But that's the number I trust. The other one was measuring how good I am at using information from the future.
